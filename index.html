Why Our University Needs a Clear AI Acceptable Use Policy

By Salva • IT Student Perspective • Academic & Information Security Policy

Introduction

If you ask students in the computer lab how they normally study, the classic answers used to be watching tutorials or reviewing notes in the library. Today, that habit has shifted, and some students rely on AI chatbots as their learning tool. They use those platforms to fix errors in their code for their programming or summarize a lesson that is difficult to understand. As an IT student, I think the university needs a clear Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) to show us where that boundary is before we start learning. This policy should protect student data, help instructors grade real learning, and cover everyone on campus including students, faculty, and staff whenever they use AI for schoolwork.

When we look at campus AI through the CIA Triad, it is easy to see where things can go wrong:

CONFIDENTIALITY: A student might copy and paste an email from the registrar or upload a project document containing group members' student numbers just to ask a chatbot for a quick summary. Public AI tools often store those prompts or use them to train future models, meaning private school records leave the university's control. So everyone should strip out personal details, use dummy text, or stick only to university approved AI tools.
INTEGRITY: AI tools are designed to sound convincing even when they invent fake research papers, generate flawed Python functions, or distort historical facts. Students must verify all information against credible class readings and compile or test their own code before submitting. Instructors also need honest disclosure whenever AI plays a role in shaping an assignment.
AVAILABILITY: If a professor requires students to use a specific third party AI website for a lab, an unexpected outage, daily prompt limit, or sudden paywall could lock half the class out of their work. No student should have to pay for a subscription to complete coursework, so teachers must always provide a free alternative through the LMS or printed worksheets.

Entering personal or school data into public AI tools also creates serious privacy risks. Once private information is typed into an unapproved chatbot, the university loses control over where that data goes, who sees it, or if it leaks later. Under the Philippines' Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173), student records, grades, and personal details count as sensitive personal information that need a valid legal reason and strong protection before being processed (Data Privacy Act of 2012). A teacher cannot paste a class grade sheet into a free chatbot to write feedback faster, and a capstone group cannot upload survey answers with real names and emails. The school must have a clear legal basis, tell students how their info is used, and share only the bare minimum data needed. UNESCO's guide on generative AI in education says the same thing, reminding schools to check if an AI tool actually helps learning and keeps student privacy safe before using it in class (Miao & Holmes, 2023).

To avoid confusion, I think the policy should give everyone a clear role using the govern, map, measure, and manage steps from NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (Tabassi, 2023). I just need to follow each task's AI rules, be honest when we use AI, double-check our work, and report accidental data leaks to the help desk right away. Teachers should explain what AI help is allowed, grade our work themselves, and keep papers with our names out of random websites. Meanwhile, the IT Office and Data Protection Officer (DPO) check apps for security and privacy and maintain a list of safe campus tools. A campus AI committee should approve new tools only after IT and the DPO clear them, while student cheating goes to the Academic Integrity Office, staff issues go to human resources, and IT with the DPO handles any leaked data.

Solution

To turn these ideas into actual rules, I think the school needs a simple policy so nobody has to guess what is allowed in class. Teachers should just put the AI rules right on the assignment instructions using three clear levels. For exams and basic skill checks, no AI is allowed at all. For regular labs and papers, limited AI help is fine for brainstorming or understanding error messages, as long as we write the final work ourselves and add a quick note about how we used it. Then for advanced IT classes, open AI use makes sense when the whole point of the lab is testing prompts or building with approved AI tools.

Aside from classwork, the policy also has to set firm limits on privacy, honesty, and fairness. Nobody on campus, whether student or staff, should ever paste confidential school files, grades, passwords, or personal info like full names and student ID numbers into public chatbots. If someone wants to use an outside AI tool to summarize notes or test code, they have to take out all private info first, while anything involving actual university records should only be done on official tools approved by the IT Office and DPO. Also, since AI regularly makes up fake references and writes broken code, we still have to double-check every source and test our own programs before submitting anything. Lastly, instructors should not require paid AI plans or tools with strict daily prompt limits unless the school gives us free accounts, and they should always allow a normal non-AI option if a website crashes.

Recommendations

The policy should split AI use into three clear levels. Everyday studying like summarizing our own notes, understanding error messages, or making practice quizzes should be allowed anytime. For graded work like brainstorming ideas, checking grammar, or debugging code, we should get our teacher's permission first and add a quick note on how we used AI. Meanwhile, submitting whole essays or code written by AI, using it during exams, or pasting grades, passwords, and personal info into public chatbots should be completely banned.

Three security ideas explain why this works. Fail-safe defaults means AI is not allowed unless a teacher specifically says it is okay, which prevents accidental cheating when instructions are vague. Least privilege means students and staff only access the exact AI tools and data they need for class, keeping private school records away from public bots. Lastly, psychological acceptability keeps the rules simple enough that people actually follow them instead of sneaking around them.

Conclusion

Overall, the three recommendations are allowing AI for everyday study, requiring teacher's permission and disclosure for graded work, and strictly banning AI on closed exams or with private data. Following these three rules protects academic integrity because students still do their own thinking and stay honest about the tools they use, while keeping personal records and campus information safe from public leaks.
